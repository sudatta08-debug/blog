The Defense of Poesy, literary criticism by [Sir Philip Sidney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Sidney), was written about 1582 and published posthumously in 1595, and this is considered to be the first written literary criticism. Another edition of the work, published the same year, is titled An Apology for Poetry.

In justification, Philip says, poetry shapes the raw material furnished by history. Poetry, unlike history, and especially philosophy, not only instructs but simulates and impels, providing incentives to learning as well as the learning itself.

Sidney composed his eloquent defense of imaginative Literature against charges of time-wasting, pre-verification and allurement to vice. Writing before England's great age of poetry and drama - too early to include William Shakespeare in his criticism - he, therefore, finds English Literature sadly wanting.

Sidney now sums up what he has said in the foregoing sections of his Apology. He has established the superiority of poetry over other learning branches and shown that it both instructs and delights, and it delights by 'moving' the reader. With great skill, he has disproved the charges of its critics and proved them to be baseless. Every line of the Apology brings out Sidney's exalted conception of poetry and the full faith in its greatness and glory.

The brief synopsis of Sidney's Defense of Poesy brings out his exalted conception of the nature and function of poetry. Says Spingarn, "In all that relates to the antiquity, universality, and pre-eminence of poetry, Sidney apparently follows Minturno". Poetry, as the first lights-giver to ignorance, flourished before any other art or science. The first philosophers and historians are poets, and such supreme works as the 'Psalms of David' and the 'Dialogues of Plato' are in reality political. Among the Greeks and the Romans, the poet was regarded as a sage or prophet, and no nation, however primitive or brutal, has been without, has failed to receive delight and instruction from poetry.
